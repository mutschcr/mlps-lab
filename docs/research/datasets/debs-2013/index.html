Grand Challenge

The ACM DEBS 2013 Grand Challenge provides a common benchmark for research and industrial event-based systems using a real-world soccer match. Competitors build systems that execute complex, concurrent, real-time analytics over high-frequency position events.

Challenge submissions were required to include a six-page ACM SIG proceedings paper and a system demonstration or screencast. Submissions were peer reviewed; optional participation in the global ranking considered both correctness and throughput/latency.

Problem description

The aim is continuous, real-time analysis of a football game using streams generated by wireless sensors embedded in the shoes of players and in the ball. The tasks include running statistics, ball possession, heat maps, and shots on goal—statistics relevant to spectators, coaches and team management.

Data

The measurements were recorded by a real-time locating system at the Nuremberg Stadium. Each player wears one sensor per foot; the goalkeeper has an additional sensor on each hand; the ball contains its own sensor. Player and goalkeeper sensors produce position updates at 200 Hz, whereas the ball produces 2,000 Hz, for a total of approximately 15,000 position events per second.

Each event identifies a transmitter, its timestamp and location, velocity and acceleration in three dimensions. The coordinate origin lies in the middle of the playing field. All recorded data are ordered by timestamp.

sid, ts, x, y, z, |v|, |a|, vx, vy, vz, ax, ay, az

sid is the sensor identifier, ts is a timestamp in picoseconds, and x, y, z are millimeter coordinates. The speed magnitude |v| is expressed in µm/s and the direction components have a scale of 10,000. Consequently, the x velocity in m/s is |v| × vx × 10⁻¹⁰. Acceleration |a| is specified in µm/s², with its three directional components; gravity is not included (a stationary ball reports zero acceleration).

The game runs from timestamp 10753295594424116 to 14879639146403495. Two teams of seven players play on a half-size field over two 30-minute halves.

A separate referee-event stream records stops and resumptions of play and substitutions. The metadata files map sensors to players and players to teams. Manually annotated statistics include game interruptions, possession and shots on goal; they can be used to validate query output. The challenge assumes the input events are replayed without delays or omissions.

Challenge queries

The four queries must be evaluated concurrently. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, results are emitted as streams and should be updated as new events arrive.

Query 1 — Running analysis

Calculate the running intensity and aggregate running performance for each player. The intensity classes are standing (0–1 km/h), trot (up to 11 km/h), low-speed run (up to 14 km/h), medium-speed run (up to 17 km/h), high-speed run (up to 24 km/h) and sprint (over 24 km/h). The actual player speed is derived from both foot sensors; noise in the raw speed readings must be considered.

Current running statistics (maximum output rate 50 Hz):

ts_start, ts_stop, player_id, intensity, distance, speed

ts_start and ts_stop delimit the intensity interval; distance is the horizontal distance travelled and speed the interval-average speed.

Aggregate running statistics:

ts, player_id, standing_time, standing_distance, trot_time, trot_distance, low_time, low_distance, medium_time, medium_distance, high_time, high_distance, sprint_time, sprint_distance

Durations are in milliseconds and distances reflect each intensity class. Compute four parallel time windows: 1, 5 and 20 minutes, and the full game. The target emission rate is at most 50 Hz per window. If an intensity lasts less than one second, it is added to the next intensity that lasts at least one second; the query may therefore delay output until a reliable classification is possible.

Query 2 — Ball possession

A player gains possession when the ball is within one metre of the nearest player-foot transmitter and the player strikes it. A hit is indicated by ball acceleration of at least 55 m/s². The player retains possession until another player hits the ball, the ball leaves the pitch or play is stopped. The ball may move farther away while still remaining in that player’s possession.

The 55 m/s² threshold depends on the fitness of the players; professional matches may require thresholds near 100 m/s². A mean filter may help detect contacts.

Per-player output:

ts, player_id, time, hits

Here time is total possession duration and hits counts player contacts with the ball.

Per-team output:

ts, team_id, time, time_percent

Compute team-possession statistics over windows of 1, 5 and 20 minutes, and the full game, producing four streams. All output streams are limited to a maximum frequency of 50 Hz.

Query 3 — Heat map

For each player, calculate the proportion of time spent in each cell of a rectangular field grid. Four spatial resolutions are required: 8 × 13 (104 cells), 16 × 25 (400), 32 × 50 (1,600) and 64 × 100 (6,400). Produce separate streams for all four grid sizes and four temporal windows: 1, 5 and 10 minutes, and the full game. This results in 16 parallel streams, each updated once per second.

ts, player_id, cell_x1, cell_y1, cell_x2, cell_y2, percent_time_in_time_cell

cell_x1, cell_y1 and cell_x2, cell_y2 define the lower-left and upper-right cell corners. percent_time_in_time_cell ranges from 0 to 100 percent. Aggregates must be restarted at half-time rather than spanning the interval between halves.

Query 4 — Shot on goal

Detect shots that would hit or narrowly miss the opposition’s goal, including attempts that are blocked or saved. A shot begins when a player hits the ball with acceleration of at least 55 m/s² and the projected trajectory intersects the goal area within 1.5 seconds. The ball may be tracked after the initial strike to refine the projected trajectory.

Goal areas:

Team 1: 22578.5 < x < 29898.5, y = 33941.0, z < 2440.0
Team 2: 22560.0 < x < 29880.0, y = -33968.0, z < 2440.0

Contact distorts the ball velocity; a Kalman filter is used in preprocessing and the measurements stabilise after the strike. A shot may be detected as late as one metre of ball movement after contact. Simple linear extrapolation is an acceptable baseline, while gravity-aware or otherwise improved trajectory models are welcome.

Output:

ts, player_id, x, y, z, |v|, vx, vy, vz, |a|, ax, ay, az

Update the result stream at sensor-event frequency while the shot state is active. The state ends when the ball leaves the field or changes direction so that the goal area would no longer be hit.

Frequently asked questions

The following answers document clarifications from the 2013 challenge.

Question 1: May the irregular playing field be approximated as a rectangle?

Yes. Recommended corners: (0, 33965), (0, −33960), (52483, 33965), (52483, −33960).

Question 2: How should player speed and position be calculated with one sensor per foot?

Average the position and velocity readings from both sensors. Updating at 200 Hz is acceptable when both readings are available.

Question 3: What is the correct input stream schema?

The correct field order is sid, ts, x, y, z, |v|, |a|, vx, vy, vz, ax, ay, az. Earlier versions of the published description accidentally omitted or misordered some fields.

Question 4: How are heat-map grid parameters combined?

The combinations are 8 × 13 = 104 cells, 16 × 25 = 400, 32 × 50 = 1,600, and 64 × 100 = 6,400.

Question 5: What virtual machine configuration is required for a ranked submission?

The organisers asked for a VMDK image and planned to set uniform VM parameters for all teams. Minimum configuration: four 2.8 GHz cores, 4 GB RAM, SSD storage and 1 Gb Ethernet. Providing a VM was optional for ranking and did not affect paper acceptance.

Question 6: Why are some z positions negative?

Absolute z positioning is error-prone. Relative z-axis movements are more accurate and should be preferred.

Question 7: Where should the result streams be written?

Standard output or a file is sufficient.

Question 8: Must the input events be replayed according to their timestamps?

No; replay timing may ignore timestamps. The results must nevertheless use the original event timestamps, including for temporal windows.

Question 9: What are the exact start and end timestamps of each half?

First half: 10753295594424116–12557295594424116. Second half: 13086639146403495–14879639146403495. Near the end of the first half the ball transmitter failed for approximately 2.5 minutes (beginning around timestamp 12398…). Ball-possession and shot-on-goal results for that interval are unreliable.

Question 10: How often must results be updated?

Unless otherwise specified, the streams should update on each input event.

Question 11: Do heat-map windows span both halves?

No. Restart windows at half-time.

Question 12: May a solution exploit the fact that all data are in a stored CSV file?

No. Systems must preserve the real-time processing aspect.

Question 13: Does the one-metre player–ball proximity refer to the ball surface?

No. Measure the distance between the ball transmitter and the closest player transmitter.

Question 14: What if running intensities change repeatedly in less than a second?

Delay the decision until an intensity is observed for at least one second; shorter intervals are assigned to the next qualifying intensity.

Question 15: Does the 50 Hz requirement conflict with delays needed for running-intensity classification?

No. It defines a maximum output frequency, not a mandatory fixed frequency. Delaying output when no result is available is acceptable.

Question 16: Does the heat-map query require 34,016 events per second for all grids and windows?

Yes, that is a possible result format: (6,400 + 1,600 + 400 + 104) × 4 = 34,016. To reduce I/O overhead, each second may instead contain one larger event collecting all cells for a grid resolution.

Question 17: Why do shot-on-goal timestamps appear several seconds ahead of the match videos?

Video and sensor data are not fully time-aligned. Sensor collection begins earlier; the match itself starts at 10753295594424116.

Question 18: Why did a published Query 4 schema contain two timestamp fields?

That was a typographical error. The corrected result schema contains only one timestamp as its first field.
